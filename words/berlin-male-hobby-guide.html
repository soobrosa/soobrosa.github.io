Berlin Male Hobby Guide

> 4 JUL 2026 :: soobrosa

A guide from "just curious" to "please take my wallet away from me."

1. MODULAR SYNTHS

What You're Getting Into

Eurorack modular synthesis is essentially building your own synthesizer from individual modules, each doing one thing — generating a sound, shaping it, modulating it, sequencing it. You connect them with patch cables, and the result is a completely unique instrument that no one else in the world has. The creative freedom is intoxicating. The financial damage is real.

The Progression (a.k.a. How You Go Broke)

Phase 1 — "Just Dipping My Toes" (~€200–600) Start with a semi-modular synth. These are self-contained instruments with patch points that let you learn the concepts without committing to a full rack. The Arturia MiniBrute 2 (~€450–550) or the Cre8Audio East Beast / West Pest (~€200–250 each) are popular starting points. They work out of the box but can later be mounted in a Eurorack case. This phase is where you learn the language: oscillators, filters, envelopes, LFOs, VCAs, CV, gates.

Phase 2 — "I Need a Case" (~€1,500–3,000) You buy a powered case (the Arturia RackBrute 3U at ~€255–300 or a TipTop Audio Mantis at ~€330–370 are popular starters), and your first real modules. Mutable Instruments Plaits is one of the best and most popular oscillators in Eurorack — it originally shipped with 16 synthesis algorithms and was later updated to 24 via the December 2022 firmware 1.2 update (Émilie Gillet's parting update before Mutable closed). It's a common first oscillator for good reason. Add a filter (Doepfer A-124 Wasp or TipTop Forbidden Planet), a VCA, an envelope generator, a sequencer, and some utilities. The key is keeping a good balance of sound generators, sound modifiers, and modulators, and making each module as multi-functional as possible.

Phase 3 — "This Is My Instrument Now" (~€5,000–10,000) Bigger case. More voices. Complex oscillators like the Make Noise DPO or Instruo Cs-L (~€550–650). Dedicated effects modules, clock dividers, quantizers, a Maths (the Make Noise Maths is practically mandatory — it does everything). You start having opinions about patch cable brands.

Phase 4 — "The Wall" (~€15,000–30,000+) Multiple cases. Buchla-style stuff. You're buying modules from tiny manufacturers who hand-solder everything in a shed. You attend Superbooth religiously. You have a ModularGrid account with seventeen saved system plans.

Berlin Specifics

Berlin is the city for this hobby:

SchneidersLaden has been the beating heart of the modular scene for over 25 years, with deep expertise in Eurorack, hardware synthesizers, and sequencers. The main location relocated in 2024 to Karl-Marx-Straße 186 in Neukölln (the former Musikhaus Bading building), with themed rooms full of gear you can play. A second site at Ganghofer Straße 1 hosts polysynths and electroacoustic instruments, and the original Kottbusser Tor space remains for repairs and workshops. This is the shop. Go there first.

Patch Point — specializes in handcrafted electronic instruments (Bugbrand, Ciat-Lonbarde, Serge Modular, Fenix, Gieskes, eKalimba, Banana) at a Berlin showroom on Lenaustraße 20. Hours have varied between Mon–Thu and Mon–Fri afternoons; check the site before visiting.

Superbooth — the world's biggest synth trade show, held annually in Berlin. The 2025 edition drew 8,800+ attendees and 301 exhibitors from 31 countries; Superbooth 26 runs 7–9 May 2026 at FEZ-Berlin.

The Snob Hierarchy

Semi-modular → starter Eurorack → all-analog purist system → Buchla → custom one-off modules from a builder you met at Superbooth → you are a module builder now.

2. SPECIALTY COFFEE

What You're Getting Into

This splits neatly into two parallel obsessions: drinking great coffee out in the world, and making it at home. Both are rewarding. Both are expensive. The home game is where the real money disappears.

The Progression

Phase 1 — "Better Than Instant" (~€50–150) A Hario V60 dripper (€8), some filters (€5), a basic stovetop gooseneck kettle (~€30 generic, ~€50 for a branded one like the Hario Buono), a decent hand grinder like the Timemore C2 (~€70–80), and fresh beans from a local roaster. This is all you need to make coffee that's dramatically better than 95% of what most people drink. The V60 pour-over ritual is meditative and satisfying.

Phase 2 — "Properly Obsessed Pour-Over" (~€400–800) Upgrade the grinder to a Comandante C40 (~€269–299 for standard MK4 Nitro Blade variants — LAB Series Tigershark variants run higher) or 1Zpresso K-Ultra (~€245–260) — these hand grinders produce incredibly uniform particles. Get a Fellow Stagg EKG electric kettle with temperature control (~€155–170 for the standard, ~€195–215 for the Pro). Add a Brewista Smart Scale (~€70–90). Now you're weighing your water, timing your pours, and adjusting grind size by single clicks. You start buying single-origin beans and tracking tasting notes.

Phase 3 — "I Want Espresso" (~€1,500–3,500) This is where the money really starts flowing. A common quote among coffee geeks is that the grinder is more important than the machine itself, and this is actually true — especially for espresso. Two classic entry points: a Gaggia Classic Pro (~€499–530) or a Rancilio Silvia (~€600–700), paired with a Eureka Mignon Specialita (~€430–530) or a DF64-style grinder (~€500–600 in EU clones). You also need a tamper, WDT tool, precision basket, knock box, and scale.

Phase 4 — "Endgame Machine" (~€3,500–7,000+) The Lelit Bianca V3 (~€1,700–1,950 EU retail, deals from ~€1,575 — a dual boiler with E61 group and flow control paddle), the Decent DE1Pro (~€3,400–3,700, the tech-nerd's dream with real-time pressure/flow/temperature profiling via tablet), or the La Marzocco Linea Mini (~€5,000–5,300 in Europe, the iconic café machine shrunk for your kitchen). Pair with a Niche Zero (~€570–650) — note that the Lagom P64, long a favorite, was discontinued in early 2026 and replaced by the Lagom P80. Now you're pressure profiling your shots and discussing extraction yield percentages on Reddit.

Phase 5 — "Collector/Roaster" (~€5,000–15,000+) You start home-roasting with an Aillio Bullet R2 (~€3,390, or ~€4,290 for the R2 Pro). You're sourcing green beans directly. You have opinions about processing methods (washed vs. natural vs. anaerobic). Your kitchen counter looks like a small café.

Berlin Specifics

Berlin's specialty coffee scene is world-class:

The Barn is the most iconic name — uncompromisingly focused on single-origin, light-roasted coffee served with minimal distraction. Multiple locations across the city. Note: roasting long ago moved out of the old Schönhauser Allee 8 space (Prenzlauer Berg, and their roastery from 2012) to a larger facility on Wattstraße in Wedding, which added a production floor, bakery, and an on-site "Annex" café facing Voltastraße — that's now the closest thing to a flagship roastery experience. Schönhauser Allee 8 still operates as a café, and the original Scheunenviertel shop on Auguststraße 58 in Mitte is where it all started in 2010.

Bonanza Coffee Roasters, founded in 2006 by Kiduk Reus and Yumi Choi, is one of the great pioneers of Berlin's specialty scene. Their Kreuzberg roastery-café on Adalbertstraße 70 is gorgeous, with the Probat roaster visible to visitors. Their Gendarmenmarkt location is built around 55 m² of fine green marble flooring and worktops.

Five Elephant — renowned for both their coffee and their cheesecake. Direct-trade relationships with farmers in Ethiopia, Guatemala, and Colombia. Locations in Kreuzberg (Reichenberger Str. 101, the original flagship), Mitte (Alte Schönhauser Str. 14), and Prenzlauer Berg (Kollwitzstr. 98 and Schwedter Str. 11). Their KaDeWe café has closed.

Father Carpenter in Mitte — widely considered one of the best spots for specialty coffee, and since 2023 they've run their own independent roastery (after splitting from Fjord). Also famous for possibly the best breakfast in Berlin.

19grams — one of the original pioneers (founded 2002 as Tres Cabezas, rebranded 19grams in 2018), with four cafés today (Alex flagship, Boxhagener/Friedrichshain, Schlesi/Kreuzberg, Chaussee/Mitte) and a strong education and accessibility focus.

Fjord Coffee Roasters — founded in 2016 by Kresten Thøgersen, Morgan Love, and James Maguire, working on bridging quality coffee and sustainable production.

For equipment shopping, Berlin has several dedicated home-barista gear shops:

KaffeeKombinat Berlin in Friedrichshain (Wühlischstraße 55) — a specialist shop for home-barista equipment: espresso machines, grinders, repairs, accessories, plus hands-on training and machine introductions. They carry Rancilio, Lelit, La Pavoni, Bezzera, BFC, Rocket, and Wega, and do E61 group repairs.

Espressomeisterei in Charlottenburg — a dedicated espresso machine retailer carrying ECM, Profitec, Rocket, and Ascaso, with Eureka and Mahlkönig grinders. They also run home-barista courses and offer in-house service and repair.

Röststätte Berlin in Mitte (Ackerstraße 173) — describes itself as Berlin's largest store for coffee accessories. They carry La Marzocco, Victoria Arduino, and Mahlkönig, plus home gear from Fellow, Hario, Timemore, Brewista, Cafelat, Kinto, and Rhinowares. Showroom for testing and comparing machines, plus a full barista academy with courses for home users.

The Snob Hierarchy

Nespresso pods → French press → pour-over with pre-ground → pour-over with freshly ground → single-origin pour-over with tasting notes → espresso → pressure-profiled espresso from beans you roasted yourself → you start a roastery.

3. VINYL & HI-FI

What You're Getting Into

Two intertwined addictions: collecting records and building the system to play them on. The beauty is that a vinyl setup is a physical, tactile, visual thing — the ritual of pulling a record from its sleeve, placing the needle, and sitting with an album for 20 minutes per side is the entire point. Berlin's record scene makes this hobby especially dangerous.

The Progression

Phase 1 — "Just Getting Started" (~€450–700) A turntable with a built-in phono preamp, like the Audio-Technica AT-LP120X (~€350–400 in EU) — notable for its direct drive motor and fully adjustable tonearm, modeled on the legendary Technics SL-1200. Pair with affordable powered speakers like Edifier R1280DBs (~€120–160) or Kanto YU4 (~€400). Plug in, play records. Done. You'll immediately hear the difference from streaming.

Phase 2 — "Separates" (~€1,000–2,500) This is where hi-fi gets real. You want separate components: a proper turntable, a standalone phono preamp, an amplifier, and passive speakers. A phono stage boosts the tiny signal from the cartridge to line level — it applies the inverse of the RIAA equalization curve baked in during record cutting/mastering, and without it, your records would sound tinny and thin. A solid setup here: Rega Planar 3 turntable (~€850–1,000 with Nd3 cartridge), Cambridge Audio Alva Solo phono stage (~€199), a Cambridge AXA35 integrated amplifier (~€350), and a pair of DALI Oberon 1 bookshelf speakers (~€329–399). Note: DALI is replacing the Oberon range with the new Sonik series starting February 2026, so the Oberon 1 may be on clearance — or check the Sonik successor.

Phase 3 — "Audiophile Sickness" (~€3,000–8,000) You upgrade the turntable to a Technics SL-1500C (~€1,100–1,200, with the SL-1500CS launching March 2026), Pro-Ject X1 B (~€999), or Rega Planar 6 (~€1,500–1,700 without cartridge, ~€1,900 with Nd7). Better cartridge (moving from Moving Magnet to Moving Coil — an Ortofon 2M Blue at ~€200 or the jump to MC with the Audio-Technica AT-OC9X line, which ranges from ~€280 for the entry XEB to €800+ for the high-end XSL). Dedicated phono preamp (~€500+). Floorstanding speakers. If you actually care about listening to records, most turntables under €300 are fundamentally compromised — spending more on a proper deck lasts decades and protects your records.

Phase 4 — "The Forever System" (~€10,000–30,000+) Turntables from VPI, Rega (P8/P10), Technics SL-1200G (~€4,300, with a new Master Edition at €5,499 from January 2026). Tube amplification. Reference-grade speakers from KEF, Focal, Harbeth. You're treating your listening room acoustically. You buy a record cleaning machine (Degritter Mark II ultrasonic at ~€2,890–3,060). You argue about speaker cable materials on forums at midnight.

Berlin Specifics — Record Shops

Berlin is arguably the best city in Europe for vinyl:

Hard Wax — legendary since 1989, founded by Basic Channel producer Mark Ernestus, a crucial node in the Berlin-Detroit techno axis. It left its long-time Paul-Lincke-Ufer factory home in Kreuzberg in October 2023 (that building is being turned into luxury flats) and now sits inside the Kraftwerk complex at Köpenicker Str. 70 in Mitte, between the clubs Tresor and OHM — and one flight of stairs instead of three. Listening stations, no-nonsense vibe, incredible selection of techno, house, dub, and reggae. This is hallowed ground.

OYE Records in Prenzlauer Berg (Oderberger Straße 4) — run by DJ Delfonic and disco head Tinko, it's become central to Berlin's analog house scene. A second location at Friedelstraße 49 in northern Neukölln (the area informally called Kreuzkölln) opened in 2013. Strong in new vinyl, dance music, hip-hop, and jazz.

Spacehall in Kreuzberg (Zossener Straße 33/35) — still one of Berlin's larger record shops, genre-spanning across three connected rooms with strengths in electronic music. It trades partly on an outsized reputation these days, but the selection is broad and it's worth a dig if you're in the Bergmannkiez.

HHV Records in Friedrichshain — started as a hip-hop vinyl shop, now covers 150,000+ vinyl titles online plus sneakers and streetwear.

Rough Trade at Karl-Marx-Straße 101 in Neukölln — opened April 2024 as the legendary London shop's first continental European outpost, covering everything from post-punk to funk to electro.

Dodo Beach in Schöneberg (Vorbergstraße 8) — over 30,000 records, with strengths in indie/alternative, reggae/ska/dub, jazz/blues/country, and punk/hardcore.

Satori at Wrangelstraße 64 in Kreuzberg — the city's best jazz specialist, in a shop that doubles as a wine merchant — half jazz records, half wine, in the same homely space.

For hi-fi equipment: Berlin's dedicated hi-fi scene has taken a hit. HiFi im Hinterhof — once a beloved institution in Kreuzberg at Großbeerenstraße 65, with over 40 years of history and multiple demo studios — entered preliminary insolvency in July 2025; formal proceedings opened on 1 October 2025 and the company ceased operations on 30 September 2025. For the time being, your best bets are online retailers like Thomann (the world's largest online music gear retailer, based in Bavaria), specialist hi-fi shops that remain open (check current listings, as the landscape is shifting), and the used market. PhonoPhono in Kreuzberg (Bergmannstraße 17) is worth checking for turntable-specific gear. For serious auditions, you may need to travel to dealers outside Berlin or arrange home demos through online retailers.

The Snob Hierarchy

Bluetooth speaker → soundbar → powered speakers → passive speakers + amp → separate phono preamp → tube amp → vinyl cleaning ritual → custom isolation platform → you rearrange your entire apartment around speaker placement.

4. CYCLING

What You're Getting Into

Berlin is one of Europe's great cycling cities — flat, well-connected with bike infrastructure, and with a culture that takes cycling seriously as daily transport and as sport. The surrounding Brandenburg countryside offers hundreds of kilometers of gravel roads, forest tracks, and quiet tarmac. You'll ride your bike every single day, which makes investing in a beautiful one feel justified.

The Progression

Phase 1 — "Solid Commuter" (~€800–1,500) A quality urban/gravel-ready bike. Something like a Standert Triebwerk (Berlin-designed), 8bar Mitte, or Sour Clueless steel frame with a Shimano GRX or SRAM Apex groupset. Flat or drop bars, fender mounts, rack compatibility. This is Berlin's bread and butter — a bike that gets you to work and handles a weekend ride into Brandenburg.

Phase 2 — "Gravel Enthusiast" (~€2,500–4,500) A proper gravel bike: Canyon Grizl, Orbea Terra, or a steel option like a Bombtrack Beyond. Carbon fork, 1x drivetrain, 40mm+ tires, tubeless setup. You start doing organized rides. Berlin and Brandenburg together form one of Germany's most versatile gravel regions — pine forests, sandy tracks, lakeside trails, and wide-open fields in every direction. You join The Gravel Club's Berlin chapter (community rides hosted at S3Velo in Friedrichshain) or the Standert Saturday Social Gravel Ride from their Kreuzberg hub.

Phase 3 — "Component Obsession" (~€4,000–7,000) You upgrade everything. Hand-built wheels (from someone like Light Wolf in Dresden or a Berlin-based wheel builder). Chris King headset and bottom bracket (€200+ each, but they last forever and come in beautiful colors). Brooks Cambium or leather saddle. Ceramic bearings. Carbon seatpost. You start attending the Kolektif Bike Fair in Berlin — founded in 2019 as a successor to the former Berliner Fahrradschau, it's become the city's own cycling trade show and community gathering. Note: the 2025 edition was postponed to 2026, and as of May 2026 no firm new date has been announced — check the organizer's site before planning around it.

Phase 4 — "Custom Build" (~€5,000–10,000+) This is peak Berlin bike snobbery: a frame built for you by a local builder.

Cicli Bonanno in Kreuzberg (Görlitzer Straße 52) — Italian builder Niccolò Bonanno crafts custom steel frames with Columbus tubing and TIG welding. He's grown the operation into one of Europe's most notable custom fabrication shops, with an annual output of around 70 bikes and a small team. Cicli Bonanno is closely associated with the MoGaSt event — a single-day ride over the Mortirolo, Gavia, and Stelvio. Framesets are custom-priced; contact them directly for current pricing.

Drust Cycles — Konstantin Drust handcrafts steel frames in Berlin specializing in bikepacking and adventure builds. His distinctive frames lean toward adventure-ready machines informed by his own extensive travels through the Middle East, Asia, and Eastern Europe.

Santucci Cycles in Prenzlauer Berg (Danziger Straße 49) — classically inspired custom steel builds with elegant styling, individually built.

Big Forest Frameworks in Potsdam-Babelsberg — custom steel frames tailored to your body and riding style. They also offer a full range of frame-building courses (lugged, fillet brazed, cargo, MTB) if you want to learn the craft yourself.

Fern Fahrräder in Lichtenberg (Josef-Orlopp-Straße 56) — touring, randonneur, and allroad frames built with Italian steel tubing and fillet brazing, the kind that consistently triggers the must-have reflex.

Berlin Rides & Routes

Grunewald Loop — a 42 km gravel route right on Berlin's doorstep, weaving through trails and paths before reaching the shores of Wannsee.

Lost-in-Brandenburg Loop — a multi-day mixed-surface bikepacking route through the forests and former East German towns northeast of Berlin. Rideable on any gravel bike with 40mm+ tires. Wild camping is legal for one night in Brandenburg (§49 BbgNatSchG — excluding nature reserves, national parks, and private land without permission).

Müggelwald / Köpenicker Forst — popular community ride destinations east of the city.

Märkische Schweiz / Oderbruch — longer day rides into Brandenburg's rolling hills and river valleys.

Community

Standert Bicycles (Friedrichstraße 23a, 10969 Berlin) — Berlin bike brand with a café and cycling hub in Kreuzberg (in the FRIZZ23 development, right at the historic Kreuzberg/Mitte border). They run regular Saturday social gravel rides. Times vary seasonally: in warmer months they typically meet from 09:30 for coffee and roll out at 10:00; in winter, meet from 10:30 and roll out at 11:00. Rides typically cover ~65 km at 26–29 km/h pace. Check grouprides.cc for current schedules and sign-up.

S3Velo in Friedrichshain (Karl-Marx-Allee 76) — gravel-focused shop carrying Orbea, Stevens, Wilier, and Scott, plus custom builds. Berlin home base for The Gravel Club.

Steel Vintage Bikes in Mitte (Wilhelmstraße 91, with a second café and workshop at Auguststraße 91) — operating since 2012, it's become a destination for classic and vintage steel bicycles with a showroom of 300+ bikes, a café, road bike rentals, and organized group rides. Worth visiting even if you're not buying.

The Snob Hierarchy

City bike with basket → single-speed → road bike → gravel bike → steel gravel with nice components → full custom steel from a named Berlin builder → titanium → you build frames yourself → you organize a gravel event.

The Cross-Hobby Berlin Saturday

Here's what peak snobbish Berlin hobby life looks like:

9:00 — Wake up, grind single-origin Ethiopian beans on your Comandante, brew a V60 while the needle drops on a jazz record you found at Satori.

10:00 — Ride your Cicli Bonanno steel gravel bike to the Standert Social Ride, stop at Bonanza for an espresso.

14:00 — Post-ride flat white at Father Carpenter.

15:00 — Swing by SchneidersLaden to "just look" at that module you've been thinking about (you buy it).

16:00 — Hit Hard Wax for new 12-inches. Spend an hour at the listening station.

17:00 — Home. Put on a fresh record. Patch the new module. Make a pour-over. Stare out the window in deep satisfaction, thinking about how you've spent approximately €400 today without blinking.

Welcome to the club.
